When a full closet still feels empty

Most people only wear about 20% of what they own, but college students with large wardrobes still spend 10–20 minutes each morning deciding what to wear — often defaulting to the same outfits or rebuying items they already have.

Through 112 surveys and 17 interviews with students aged 15–26, we validated three pain points: no visibility into the wardrobe, no guidance when deciding, and too much effort to explore new styles. I helped translate those into design opportunities and built the core flows in our final prototype.

My role: As one of two UX designers, I owned the Home and Virtual Closet flows, led user flow and IA decisions, and iterated onboarding, closet views, and outfit recommendations through two rounds of usability testing.

JUMP TO FINAL SOLUTION

The Problem

How might we help students with large wardrobes see what they own, make faster outfit decisions, and explore style from clothes they already have?

We only wear about 20% of our closet, while the rest goes unused (Pareto Principle). The issue is not having too few or too many clothes — it is that the closet does not function as a system. Everything feels scattered, so you lose track of what you own and sometimes buy things you already have.

Among UW students in our study, nearly every interview participant described the same loop: forgetting items, rebuying duplicates, and falling back on a small set of safe outfits. This HMW came directly from that research — not from the product idea.

How research validated this HMW

Pain 1 · Visibility

Survey + interviews: forgetting owned items and accidental rebuying came up in nearly every session.

Design for wardrobe visibility, not just storage.

Pain 2 · Decision guidance

Participants spent 10–20 minutes choosing outfits with limited confidence in the result.

Design for faster decisions with light guidance, not more options.

Pain 3 · Style exploration

Most wanted to try new looks but said pairing outfits from existing pieces felt like too much effort.

Design for exploration from the closet users already have.

What success looked like

Decision time Reduce daily outfit decision time from ~15 min to under 5
Wardrobe visibility Help users find and recognize owned items without rebuying
Cognitive load Lower mental effort during onboarding and core tasks (NASA-TLX)
Style confidence Increase self-reported confidence in outfit choices post-test

Research

Validating the problem before designing the product

112 Survey responses
17 Interviews
15–26 Age range
50+ Avg. items owned

We focused on college-aged users at UW who shop regularly, maintain large wardrobes, and struggle with daily outfit decisions. Our survey reached 112 students; we followed up with 17 semi-structured interviews (30–45 min each) to understand how they shop, organize, and get dressed.

We mapped responses across three areas — closet organization, outfit decisions, and style exploration — and looked for patterns strong enough to shape the product direction.

1. Struggle to keep track of what’s in their closet

When people cannot easily see what they have, they forget items and rebuy duplicates. This was the most frequent theme across survey open responses and interviews.

2. Lack of assurance with their current style

Most participants spent 10–20 minutes deciding what to wear each morning, but still left unsure whether the outfit fit the occasion or their personal style.

3. Overwhelmed by the effort needed to try new styles

Participants wanted to explore new looks, but pairing outfits from existing pieces felt like too much work — so they returned to the same safe combinations.

Most apps focus on separate features, not a connected experience

While many apps help you organize your closet or find inspiration, they rarely connect those two. This leaves you with no real support when you're actually making style decisions in the moment.

So turning that inspiration into a real outfit using what you already own, or truly defining your personal style remains a frustrating challenge.

From validated pains to design opportunities

Each insight mapped to a specific opportunity area. Our goal was not to build another closet organizer — it was to turn validated pain points into a connected system for visibility, guidance, and exploration.

Pain Point 1

No visibility into wardrobe

Opportunity Area 1

Make the wardrobe visible and easy to organize

Pain Point 2

No time and no guidance

Opportunity Area 2

Provide guidance while saving time in decision making

Pain Point 3

Limited ways to explore personal style

Opportunity Area 3

Help users discover style preferences through their existing wardrobe

Ideation

Turning validated pains into product decisions

To ground our ideas, we created Jacqueline, a persona based on interview patterns — overwhelmed by a cluttered wardrobe, forgetting what she owns, and defaulting to the same outfits.

Using her journey as our guide, I clustered ideas into three themes tied to our validated pains: making the wardrobe visible, reducing daily decision effort, and exploring style from existing pieces.

This led us to define a clear information architecture with five main sections: Home, Virtual Closet, Explore, Archive, and Stats (which later integrated into ‘My Page’). With these pillars in place, we moved straight into sketching for each tab.

Mapping the flow and framework

Since the Home page is the first thing users see, I focused on helping them decide today’s outfit quickly and with less effort. Whether they prefer selecting manually, picking from saved outfits, or getting AI-driven recommendations, the flow is designed to minimize cognitive load.

For recommendations, our research showed that users primarily consider occasion and weather when choosing outfits, so we tailored suggestions based on those factors, along with their preferred style.

In the low-fidelity prototypes, I surfaced the daily forecast at the top and added a weekly calendar on the bottom so users can track what they wore, recognize patterns over time, and plan ahead for special occasions.

After putting together the low-fi in light mode, we felt like everything looked a bit flat and the clothes didn’t really stand out. Since the whole point of the app is to focus on outfits, we decided to switch to dark mode to make the clothing pop more.

We also chose blue as our primary color to make sure the UI feels like a natural extension of your clothes rather than a distraction, since blue is one of the most universal colors in any closet (outside of neutrals). With this visual direction set, we started building out our design system and moving into high-fidelity prototyping.

Four flows built to solve three validated pains

Rather than pitching a virtual closet, I designed four flows — each tied to a research pain point, tested through two rounds of usability testing, and iterated based on NASA-TLX scores and participant feedback.

01 Home

Tracking Outfits And Recognizing Style Patterns

Pain: Users could not see patterns in what they actually wear.

My decision: I designed Home around daily outfit logs instead of asking users to define their style upfront.

Outcome: Recurring patterns surface through behavior, helping users reflect without extra effort.

02 Get Outfit Recs

Guiding Decisions Without Adding Choices

Pain: Too many options increased decision fatigue instead of reducing it.

My decision: I redesigned recommendations to show one outfit at a time with a simple accept-or-pass interaction.

Outcome: Users made faster decisions without comparing multiple options at once.

03 Virtual Closet

Balancing Visibility And Information Density

Pain: Users forgot what they owned because the full wardrobe felt visually overwhelming.

My decision: I added collapsible categories and switchable grid/list views to improve visibility without overload.

Outcome: Users could find items faster while still seeing their full wardrobe when needed.

04 Explore Styles

Connecting Inspiration With What You Own

Pain: Inspiration lived on Pinterest, but translating it into owned pieces was too much work.

My decision: I designed Explore to match inspiration outfits with similar items already in the closet.

Outcome: Users could try new looks without buying new clothes.

Validation

Validating the problem and the design

Problem validation

Before building features, we validated that the problem was real. Across 112 surveys and 17 interviews, the same three themes kept appearing — wardrobe visibility, decision guidance, and style exploration — which became our HMW and design principles.

Design validation

After our first iteration, I helped run two rounds of usability testing with 10 participants. We used the NASA Task Load Index What is NASA Task Load Index(NASA-TLX)? A method where people rate mental effort, stress, and performance of the task (1–10 scale) to measure cognitive load, and followed up with a post-test survey on confidence and ease of use.

Reflection

What I learned as a designer

This project taught me to lead with research, not features. The strongest work came from validating pains first, translating them into a focused HMW, and being willing to cut ideas that did not map back to what users actually struggled with.

I also learned to own decisions end to end — from IA and user flows to usability iterations. Testing onboarding, closet density, and recommendation patterns helped me move from "a closet app" to a system that addressed real decision friction.

Given More Time

During our interviews, we noticed that outfit decisions can be shaped by race and culture. For some participants, factors like modesty played a big role in how they choose what to wear. We started a second round of interviews with a more diverse group but could not explore this further due to time constraints. I would want to dive deeper to design a more inclusive experience.

Possible Next Step

Right now, the experience focuses on a relatively closed system — your own wardrobe. As a next step, I would explore connecting external inspiration sources like fashion communities or lookbooks with wardrobe data, so users can expand their style without buying new clothes.

Outcome

At the DFA UW Showcase in May 2025, our project was selected as Best Overall Design among six teams, judged by three industry professionals. It felt like recognition of both the solution and how clearly we communicated the problem and design process.
